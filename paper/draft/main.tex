\title[Clock-Sensitive Waits and Fault-Aware Repair]{Clock-Sensitive Waits and Fault-Aware Repair in Zephyr RTC Tests}
\author{Pranav Krishnan}
\affiliation{\institution{The University of Texas at Austin}\city{Austin}\state{Texas}\country{USA}}
\author{Keshav Bhargava}
\affiliation{\institution{The University of Texas at Austin}\city{Austin}\state{Texas}\country{USA}}

\begin{abstract}
Firmware tests often wait a fixed time for a hardware event. A slower but datasheet-legal clock can make a correct program fail that test. We study clock-sensitive waits and fault-aware repair using one Zephyr STM32 real-time clock (RTC) driver and the existing Renode model. Three selected upstream cases and three generated suites fail at legal clock settings; four repairs make each pass all 102 tested settings while retaining measured slow-fault detection. Separately, three repairs make a fourth upstream counter case pass all 102 settings after 44 original failures. Its 10-kHz fault result remains unqualified: an independent progress control exceeds its preregistered timing bound by 18 ms. The full counter application still has short-relative and single-shot failures. Right-sizing overlong deadlines adds 11 slow-fault detections in different generated counting tests; polling adds none on common-evaluable pairs. In 180 fixed-setting reruns, each of six cells has one consistent verdict, so we observe timing sensitivity across settings but no same-setting flakiness in those cells. We report these outcomes as a case study of one shared simulator model, not a developer-test prevalence or general repair rate.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{embedded testing, timing sensitivity, test repair, RTC, fault detection}
\maketitle

\section{Introduction}
An RTC test schedules an alarm ten calendar seconds ahead, waits thirteen simulated (Renode virtual) seconds, and asserts that a callback arrived. Its wait assumes that the RTC clock and callback path fit the observation window. At a slower but datasheet-legal clock, the alarm target itself may occur after the assertion. Extending the wait can fix the legal-clock failure. An excessively long deadline can also accept a faulty clock, so a passing repaired test alone is insufficient.

Our question is: \textbf{when legal clock variation changes a test verdict, can we repair the timing assumption without weakening measured fault detection?} We analyze paired repair records for one Zephyr STM32 RTC driver in Renode. The original fault-qualified result concerns six subjects in Table~\ref{tab:subjects}. A separate full-application counter extension tests a fourth upstream waiting-sensitive case; its legal repair succeeds, but its slow-fault qualification remains unresolved. We also distinguish deadline sizing, state polling and added alarm checks.

\textbf{Connection to flaky tests.} Timing assumptions can make tests unreliable across operating conditions~\cite{luo2014flaky,strandberg2020,rahman2024flakerake}. Here, a consistent pass at one fixed frequency and failure at another is \emph{timing sensitivity}, not observed nondeterminism at one setting. We also ran 30 fresh processes in each of six fixed test-frequency cells: all 180 verdicts agree within their cells. This pilot finds no same-setting flakiness in those cells; it cannot rule out rare flakiness elsewhere. We did not measure a device-frequency population distribution or sample developer tests randomly.

\section{Approach: Repair the Wait, Recheck the Oracle}
\subsection{A clock-derived deadline}
The pinned Zephyr v4.3.0 source~\cite{zephyrpinned} selects the low-speed internal oscillator (LSI) for the STM32F4 Discovery RTC. DS8626 Rev 12 lists 17-kHz minimum, 32-kHz typical and 47-kHz maximum at 3 V and $-40$ to $105\,^{\circ}$C~\cite{stm32f407datasheet}. We test both endpoints and the nominal typical rate; the datasheet does not give their population probabilities. With a fixed $128\times250$ prescaler, ten RTC seconds ideally take
\[
 t(f)=320{,}000/f\quad\text{simulated seconds, with }f\text{ in Hz}.
\]
At 17 kHz this is 18.824 s, beyond the original 13-second wait. Its ideal boundary is 24.615 kHz. These are manually source-derived thresholds, not an automatically inferred specification; full-application phase effects can require a band rather than one boundary.

The repair ceiling is \textbf{20.824 simulated seconds}: the ideal slow-clock duration, 18.823529\ldots{} s, rounded up to the next millisecond (18.824 s), plus \textbf{two seconds of unvalidated experimental allowance}. It is not a measured or guaranteed interrupt-to-callback latency bound. Kernel uptime measures the wait while CPU/SysTick remain fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} makes the original waiting assumption concrete.

In the upstream callback source, lines 120 and 132 sleep for constants of three and ten seconds; lines 126--129 check for premature callbacks and lines 140--141 require arrival. V1/V1b retain the early check and sleep only until the revised deadline; V2 can finish when the completion predicate becomes true. At the injected 10-kHz slow fault, the same target ideally takes 32 s, so both the original and repaired deadlines reject it. This explains why extending this subject's wait can preserve its slow-fault check.

\subsection{Paired mechanical variants}
\textbf{V0} is the original test. \textbf{V1}, naive extension, uses the larger of the original wait and 20.824 s; it never shortens an overlong wait. \textbf{V1b}, right-sizing, uses a 20.824-second total ceiling while preserving the original fixed-sleep or polling strategy and cadence. \textbf{V2} uses the same ceiling and original completion predicate with 20-ms state polling, latching destructive pending reads. \textbf{V3} adds missing checks from the frozen task specification: supported alarm-field readback, callback identity/calendar checks, or counting progress/monotonicity as applicable. This deliberately augments upstream oracles. Original targets, early checks and non-time assertions remain. V1 preserves existing service stages (Sol pending r03 can reach 23.824 s); V1b--V3 share one total ceiling. The dated transformation rules were fixed before repair builds.

The later full-application counter extension has its own frozen deadline: V1 waits 13 s, V1b waits 7.648 s, and V2 polls the original callback count for at most 7.648 s. V3 and calendar-alarm offsets do not apply to this relative-counter case. We compare all three variants with archived V0 verdicts at the same 100 frequencies and 17/47-kHz endpoints; we do not pool them with the 33-subject cohort.

Faults are tested separately: fast 64-kHz and slow 10-kHz oscillator inputs, a nominal-clock \emph{nonarrival fault} suppressing alarm delivery or freezing counting reads, and nominal-clock alarm-compare offsets of $-1$ or $+1$ RTC second. A table \emph{kill} means an activated fault detected by an explicit selected-case assertion. Alarm programming, calendar-target arrival and callback delivery are different properties: readback checks programming; calendar equality also assumes delivery before the calendar advances. No delivery-delay experiment was performed.

\section{Experimental Setup and Scope}
\textbf{Scope.} The original comparison purposively selected three upstream cases for visible timing windows: alarm callback, alarm pending and calendar counting. The later full-application expansion contains \textbf{10 distinct runnable upstream case names}, including those three, from 19 declared names: five runnable RTC API cases, four counter basic cases and one counter seconds case. Four distinct names have waiting-sensitive failures; the fourth, counter all-channels, was repaired later in its full application. These are simulated board configurations, not physical boards. They share \textbf{one existing Renode STM32F4 RTC model}, so board labels do not provide independent hardware replication. Counter applications use \textbf{non-stock RTC-only configurations}, with the existing counter adapter enabled and other timer counters and the RTC API driver disabled; they do not add a second repaired driver. Capability predicates are retained. Neither this selection nor the repair subset estimates developer-test prevalence.

\textbf{Execution.} Renode v1.17.0 is an instruction-level embedded simulator~\cite{renodetime}. Each run starts a fresh process with unchanged source/ELF, CPU, SysTick and synchronization quantum within its variant. The frozen schedule has 100 shared integer-frequency settings plus two endpoints within the documented interval. Build, five nominal passes and small calibration precede production; the Linux container was calibrated against local verdicts and trace hashes. A dated extension built three counter-test variants and ran their full applications on the same schedule. A separate diagnostic job replayed selected failures and ran clean-state references and oscillator-progress controls; none replaced a primary verdict.

F4, F767 and F746 use 17--47 kHz; F767 is explicitly an F4 configuration replica. H753 uses the frozen DS12117 Rev 10 revision-V envelope, 29.760--33.600 kHz at VDD 1.62--3.6 V and junction $-40$ to $105\,^{\circ}$C~\cite{stm32h753datasheet}. F746 counter basic is excluded after an MPU fault, F072 after no test output, and watchdog after no demonstrated reboot. The F072 divider observation remains unconfirmed.

\textbf{Attribution and missingness.} Same-input failure replays, exact source lines and task-specific references distinguish waiting defects, phase-dependent waits, cleanup cascades and unresolved rate contracts. References check premature behavior and progress under their registered ceiling; a passing progress reference cannot override a failed rate reference. Capability skips and missing verdicts remain separate from passes. Infrastructure-interrupted evaluations without a verdict were resumed under a dated amendment, while finalized harness-error outcomes remain missing.

\textbf{Repair cohort.} The original roster was fixed after the initial clock study and before repair builds: three isolated upstream cases plus 30 generated scalar-budget suites (27 counting, three pending), with no generated callback suite. This selection favors identifiable waiting budgets and is not a general repair-success sample. Each variant has 3,300 seeded legal slots and 66 endpoints; alarm offsets apply to five alarm subjects. Paired comparisons use only subjects evaluable in both variants. The later counter extension is one additional upstream case in a full application, reported separately.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Six sensitive subjects: repaired waits retain slow-fault detection}
\textbf{Each of the six subjects passes the tested legal settings after every repair and retains its measured slow-fault detection.} Table~\ref{tab:subjects} shows the three upstream subjects and three generated pending suites separately. Every repaired subject passes all 102 legal settings with zero missing verdicts. Naive extension already achieves this result: it does \emph{not} weaken slow-fault detection in these six subjects.

As supporting detail, the six subjects contribute 612 shared subject-setting pairs: V0 produces 500 passes and 112 failures; each repair produces 612 passes. These are \textbf{six subjects, not 612 independent tests}. Their repeated clock settings describe each subject's sensitivity. The 112 original failing slots have passing task-specific references and become observed passes under all repairs. This result concerns the original isolated contexts, not all expanded case names or untested clock settings.

\subsection{Fourth upstream case: legal repair, unresolved fault control}
The full F4 counter application exposes a distinct waiting defect in \texttt{counter\_basic.test\_all\_channels}. Its original three-second busy-wait fails at 44 of 102 tested legal frequencies, including 17 kHz, and passes at the nominal 32 kHz and 47 kHz. The driver may add a compensating tick to its two-tick relative alarm; at the 17-kHz endpoint, three ticks ideally need 5.647 s before callback delivery. The prespecified 13-s extension, 7.648-s right-sized wait and 7.648-s polling deadline each pass the same 102 settings (Table~\ref{tab:counter}). This is \textbf{one additional upstream case}, not 306 independent repairs or a second driver.

The full application does not become failure-free. The original all-channels failure skipped the later short-relative case at 44 settings. Once the repaired wait lets it run, short-relative fails at 14 settings. The later single-shot case fails at 15 settings after repair, down from 44 original failures: 29 original failures disappear on paired runs, while 15 remain. We do not count these as additional repaired tests or label every residual failure a cleanup cascade. Seventeen representative full-application replays match the primary case vectors and traces. Three clean-state functional references pass at 17 legal settings each, covering all 87 residual failing case slots across variants; they do not reproduce the original application's phase and order.

The raw selected 10-kHz verdict is fail in V0, pass in V1, and fail in V1b/V2; all four 64-kHz selected probes pass. These outcomes replay, but the separate 10-kHz progress control measures 16.040 s for five ticks, \textbf{18 ms above its preregistered 16.022-s upper bound}. Its 32- and 64-kHz controls meet their bounds. Source and saved traces do not isolate the extra 18 ms, so \textbf{slow-fault activation and preservation remain unresolved for this fourth case}. We retain the failed control instead of widening its bound after observation. No new nonarrival or offset probe was qualified for this case.

\subsection{A separate result: right-sizing over-padded counting tests}
\textbf{Right-sizing adds 11 slow-fault detections in different, already clock-tolerant generated counting subjects.} Across the full 33-subject cohort, V1 retains V0's 21/33 slow-fault detections because it preserves long waits. V1b detects 32/32 evaluable slow faults: the 32 common V1/V1b pairs retain 21 detections, gain 11 and lose none. All 11 gains are generated counting subjects with 102 V0 legal passes and no missing legal verdicts. They correct over-padding; they are \emph{not} the six sensitive subjects in Table~\ref{tab:subjects}.

V0 has 106 seeded and six endpoint failures. Every repair has zero observed legal failures across the cohort, but V1/V1b/V2/V3 retain respectively 7/5/3/2 missing seeded outcomes; all 66 endpoints are observed passing. There are 29 finalized missing outcomes across legal and fault probes. They are not counted as successes. All 458 primary assertion failures replay exactly, including raw trace hashes.

\subsection{Polling and added alarm checks do different work}
\textbf{Polling adds no paired slow-fault detection, while extra alarm checks detect a previously accepted programming error.} V2 detects the slow fault in 33/33 subjects, but all 32 common V1b/V2 pairs already detect it under V1b. The remaining V1b outcome is missing, so the denominator change supplies no evidence of a polling gain. Polling instead shortens the upstream callback's median seeded whole-test duration from 42.0 s (V1b) to 20.75 s (V2). The pooled median is 11.0 s for every variant and hides this difference; whole-test time is not callback latency.

V0--V2 detect each alarm-offset sign in 4/5 applicable subjects. V3 reaches 5/5 for each sign by adding detection in the upstream callback. Readback and callback-calendar checks were introduced together; these records cannot isolate timestamp checking's effect. Across adjacent variants, no detected fault becomes accepted on common-evaluable subject pairs. Nonarrival is detected in 32/32 evaluable V0 subjects and 33/33 after repair; its missing V0 verdict is not a paired gain. No evaluable fast-oscillator probe is detected (0/32 for V1b, 0/33 otherwise). Preservation of this measured fault set therefore does not establish a complete oracle.

Per simulated configuration, waiting-sensitive names are 4/10 runnable names on F4 and its F767 replica, 3/6 on F746, and 0/10 on H753. These are within-cohort fractions, not population prevalence estimates.

Pending has the ideal 24.615-kHz boundary. F4 full-application callback failures span sampled 17.000--23.673 kHz (passes 23.847--47.000); counting failures span 17.000--20.500 (passes 20.701--47.000). The counter all-channels wait has a source-derived phase envelope of 21.333--32.000 kHz. Relative to isolated runs, F4 full/isolated seeded failure counts are pending 26/26, callback 22/26 and counting 12/17. Phase carryover and ordering are a source-supported explanation, \emph{not directly measured phase}.

A later counter single-shot setup failure follows skipped cleanup and is a cascade, not a fifth independent waiting-sensitive name. Counter seconds fails over sampled 17.000--26.168 kHz and passes 27.086--47.000, but its 105 rate references also fail while progress checks pass. It remains a firmware/simulator/accuracy-contract investigation, not a demonstrated waiting defect. \subsection{Secondary generated-test context}
The generated cohort covers only the original three task purposes: \texttt{gpt-6-\allowbreak{}luna}, \texttt{gpt-6-\allowbreak{}sol} and \texttt{gpt-6-\allowbreak{}astra} each produced five source-only ztests (Zephyr's test framework) per task and prompt. Of 90 attempts, 66 pass functional/build/five-nominal gates. The base prompt omits numeric clock ranges; the other adds ranges and a delivery reminder. Suite-level clock sensitivity is 3/34 base versus 0/32 with ranges (two-sided Fisher $p=0.239$); 12 original seeded verdicts remain missing. Models received purpose-derived specifications, not upstream code or execution feedback. The specifications requested stronger observables, including configured-time callbacks/IDs and pending readback/not-before checks. With no same-spec human baseline, neither oracle differences nor this small prompt contrast establishes model superiority or an isolated numeric-range effect. Detailed generation, yield, two-axis, fault and distribution tables are retained in the artifact rather than expanding this comparison here.

\textbf{Fixed-setting repeatability.} We reran unchanged full applications in 30 fresh processes per cell. The F4 callback fails 30/30 at 23.673 kHz and passes 30/30 at 23.847 and 32 kHz. F4 counter all-channels fails 30/30 at 29.143 kHz and passes 30/30 at 29.352 and 32 kHz. All 180 outcomes are explicit and agree with archived same-frequency vectors. This pilot shows no mixed verdict within these six cells; it does not establish a population flakiness rate or rule out rare nondeterminism.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{What the repair evidence supports.} The original six-subject result shows that those waits accept tested legal clocks while retaining measured slow-fault detection. The fourth upstream counter case adds legal-clock repair evidence in a full application, with residual failures exposed by the repair and an unresolved slow-fault control. The different 11-subject counting result shows why preserving an already overlong wait can leave a weak slow-clock oracle. Right-sizing supplies those gains without polling; polling reduces time; augmented checks improve alarm-offset detection. None establishes semantic equivalence for every defect or an automatic general repair procedure.

\textbf{Clock ranges and distributions.} The pinned HSI (high-speed internal oscillator) context study varied 15.84--16.16 MHz ($\pm1\%$ at $25\,^{\circ}$C), with integer 99--101-MIPS CPU mapping: no failures among 943 upstream and 3,270 generated evaluable seeded runs; 87 seeded outcomes were not evaluable. Different tasks, the narrow range and throughput approximation prevent a direct HSI-versus-LSI causal comparison. The RTC study isolates LSI variation with fixed CPU/SysTick; its 17--47-kHz range is much wider. The HSI result does not imply HSI can never matter.

Neither uniform sampling nor a datasheet interval measures how frequencies are distributed across devices. We lead with boundaries/bands, retaining uniform and legal-conditioned normal sensitivity calculations in the artifact as illustrative assumptions only. ST documents describe oscillator accuracy/calibration, not a usable F407 population distribution for this analysis~\cite{st_an5067,st_an5126,st_rm0090}. The board survey's 106 LSI versus 40 LSE (external-crystal) RTC-enabled targets supplies context, not a flaky-test prevalence estimate.

\textbf{Practical guidance and limits.} Derive waits from the worst-case legal clock, justify a delivery allowance, retain early-arrival checks against an independent time reference, and assert the RTC calendar/programming target rather than eventual arrival alone. Our extra two seconds is an unvalidated experimental allowance: no explicit interrupt-to-callback latency bound was found in 33 scanned files. The counter control starts its uptime clock before its initial counter read and polls every 20 ms; saved traces sample only every 0.5 s. These observations suggest an observer-timing explanation for the 18-ms discrepancy, but do not measure its source or satisfy the frozen bound. A future delivery bound cannot be inferred from these records. Fast-fault blindness, purposive selection, shared model behavior and non-stock counters limit external validity. Source/padding coding used one unblinded reviewer; related suites and shared frequencies are not independent replication. The artifact's $2\times$ padding cutoff is descriptive, not a correctness threshold. Missing verdicts remain missing.

\section{Related Work}
Flaky-test studies report asynchronous waits and environment assumptions~\cite{luo2014flaky,strandberg2020}; FlakeRake perturbs timing to reproduce intermittent failures~\cite{rahman2024flakerake}. Our clock changes expose sensitivity across settings, while the six fixed-setting pilot cells show no within-cell mixed verdicts. Interrupt-driven testing likewise explores scheduling conditions~\cite{regehr2005random}. Mutation testing and oracle research motivate fault checks after a repair, rather than accepting nominal passes alone~\cite{jia2011mutation,barr2015oracle}. Our contribution is the paired separation of wait repair, deadline sizing and oracle augmentation in this RTC setup, including a counter case whose fault control limits interpretation.

\section{Conclusion}
Each of six original sensitive subjects passes its tested legal settings after repair and retains measured slow-fault detection. A fourth upstream counter case also passes all 102 tested legal settings after repair, while short-relative and single-shot failures remain in its full application and its slow-fault control remains unresolved. Separately, right-sizing improves detection in 11 already clock-tolerant counting subjects. These results support targeted clock-sensitive wait repair with explicit limits on faults and the shared simulator model.
