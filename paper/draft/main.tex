\title[Clock-Sensitive Waits and Fault-Aware Repair]{Clock-Sensitive Waits and Fault-Aware Repair in Zephyr RTC Tests}
\author{Pranav Krishnan}
\affiliation{\institution{The University of Texas at Austin}\city{Austin}\state{Texas}\country{USA}}
\author{Keshav Bhargava}
\affiliation{\institution{The University of Texas at Austin}\city{Austin}\state{Texas}\country{USA}}

\begin{abstract}
A longer wait can remove a test failure while weakening its fault detection. We study this trade-off in one Zephyr STM32 RTC setup using Renode's existing model. In a full-application counter case, a naive 13-s extension makes the edited case pass every tested legal clock setting but accepts a 10-kHz slow-clock fault; a shorter clock-derived 7.648-s deadline removes the same legal failures and rejects that fault in the tested execution context. A prespecified sweep of seven waits over 14 clock rates shows a monotonic trade-off: the waits accept 0 to 8 of 12 out-of-envelope slow rates, and right-sizing still accepts three. Every cell predicted to be all-accept or all-reject matches, but all phase-dependent cells reject, so the sampled boundaries sit at the detection-favorable edge; phase was not controlled and detection across phases is not established. Downstream application failures remain. Six other sensitive subjects pass after repair and retain detection at 10 kHz, as expected from their deadline arithmetic. These manually derived variants distinguish deadline sizing, state polling and added oracle checks; none detects fast-clock faults. Fixed-setting repeats show no observed within-cell flakiness. The findings are limited to selected tests and one simulator model; silicon fidelity and practical CI incidence remain unvalidated.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{embedded testing, timing sensitivity, test repair, RTC, fault detection}

\section{Introduction}
The counter test in our study requests a two-tick alarm, waits three simulated (Renode virtual) seconds and requires a callback. Legal slow-clock settings can delay that event past the assertion. Extending the wait to thirteen seconds removes the selected case's legal-clock failures, but also accepts an injected 10-kHz clock. A 7.648-second deadline removes the same legal failures while rejecting that fault in the tested full-application context. At 10 kHz, the ideal two-to-three-tick arrival spans 6.4--9.6 s, straddling that deadline: the result does not establish detection for every phase. This concrete trade-off motivates our question: \textbf{when legal clock variation changes a test verdict, can we repair the timing assumption without weakening measured fault detection?}

We study one Zephyr STM32 RTC setup in Renode. The counter result is one additional upstream subject in a full application, whose other cases still fail. We contrast it with six originally sensitive subjects for which retaining 10-kHz detection follows the deadline arithmetic, and with different generated counting tests whose original waits were already overlong. The repairs are manually derived, rule-based variants, not an automated repair algorithm or evidence of a general repair-success rate.

\textbf{Research questions.} RQ1: Which legal-clock failures disappear after a wait repair, and which application failures remain? RQ2: How do deadline sizing, polling and added checks change paired fault detection and duration? RQ3: How do observed fault outcomes compare with clock-derived timing bounds?

\textbf{Contributions.} We provide (1) paired legal/fault evidence for a counter trade-off in its full application, (2) a subject-level separation of six sensitive tests from 11 already-tolerant counting tests, and (3) source-derived acceptance bands, compared with a sampled seven-wait, 14-rate sweep, that explain the slow-fault trade-off and fast-fault blindness. The sweep samples one uncontrolled phase per cell, not phase coverage. The contribution is a bounded empirical case study, not a new repair algorithm.

\textbf{Connection to flaky tests.} Timing assumptions can make tests unreliable across operating conditions~\cite{luo2014flaky,strandberg2020,rahman2024flakerake}. Here, a consistent pass at one fixed frequency and failure at another is \emph{timing sensitivity}, not observed nondeterminism at one setting. Section~\ref{sec:repeat} finds no same-setting flakiness in six fixed cells. We did not measure a device-frequency population distribution or sample developer tests randomly.

\section{Approach: Repair the Wait, Recheck the Oracle}
\subsection{Deriving the counter deadline}
The pinned Zephyr v4.3.0 source~\cite{zephyrpinned} selects the low-speed internal oscillator (LSI) for the STM32F4 Discovery RTC. DS8626 Rev 12 specifies 17--47 kHz with 32 kHz typical at 3 V and $-40$ to $105\,^{\circ}$C~\cite{stm32f407datasheet}. With prescaler $P=128\times250=32000$, a counter tick ideally takes $P/f$ independent seconds. The selected case requests $n=2$ relative ticks; the driver adds one tick to compensate a partly elapsed second. Its ideal delay is therefore between $nP/f$ and $(n+1)P/f$.

For minimum legal frequency $f_{\min}$ and allowance $A$, the right-sized deadline in milliseconds is
\[
D_{\rm ms}=\left\lceil1000\left((n+1)P/f_{\min}+A\right)\right\rceil.
\]
For $n=2$, $f_{\min}=17000$ Hz and $A=2$ s, this gives 7648 ms. Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the \emph{evaluated} literal substitution: it is a build-time specialization of this formula, not a portable runtime implementation. A general implementation would obtain the tick target, prescaler and legal minimum from the configuration and handle units and overflow; we have not evaluated that implementation. The two seconds are an \textbf{unvalidated experimental allowance}, not a guaranteed interrupt-to-callback bound. Kernel uptime measures the wait with CPU/SysTick fixed.

\paragraph{The separate callback deadline.}
The original three-task cohort uses a ten-RTC-second target, ideally $320000/f$ simulated seconds. At legal 17 kHz this takes 18.824 s; its repair ceiling is \textbf{20.824 s}, rounding upward to milliseconds and adding the same two-second allowance. The callback's \emph{original} 13-s wait (source lines 120 and 132; distinct from the counter's \emph{extended} 13-s wait) fails at 17 kHz and passes at 32 kHz; its ideal boundary is 24.615 kHz. At 10 kHz the target ideally takes 32 s, exceeding both callback deadlines and explaining preserved detection.

\subsection{Paired mechanical variants}
\textbf{V0} is the original test. \textbf{V1}, naive extension, uses the larger of the original wait and 20.824 s; it never shortens an overlong wait. \textbf{V1b}, right-sizing, uses a 20.824-second total ceiling while preserving the original fixed-sleep or polling strategy and cadence. \textbf{V2} uses the same ceiling and original completion predicate with 20-ms state polling, latching destructive pending reads. \textbf{V3} adds missing checks from the task specification: supported alarm-field readback, callback identity/calendar checks, or counting progress/monotonicity as applicable. This deliberately augments upstream oracles. Original targets, early checks and non-time assertions remain. V1 preserves existing service stages (one generated pending test can reach 23.824 s); V1b--V3 share one total ceiling. Transformation rules were specified before repair builds.

The later full-application counter extension has its own deadline: V1 waits 13 s, V1b waits 7.648 s, and V2 polls the original callback count for at most 7.648 s. The 13-s value was inherited before the repair runs from a conservative counter reference: $\lceil4\times32000/17000+5\rceil=13$ s, allowing an extra uncertainty tick and five seconds. It is not an optimal or representative developer choice; Section~\ref{sec:sweep} measures alternative waits. The 7.648-s bound instead rounds $3\times32000/17000+2$ upward to milliseconds. V3 and calendar-alarm offsets do not apply. We compare these variants with archived V0 verdicts at the same 102 settings, separately from the 33-subject cohort.

We test faults separately: fast 64-kHz and slow 10-kHz oscillator inputs, a nominal-clock \emph{nonarrival fault} that suppresses alarm delivery or freezes counting reads, and nominal-clock alarm-compare offsets of $-1$ or $+1$ RTC second. A \emph{detection} means an activated fault detected by an explicit selected-case assertion. The out-of-envelope oscillator probes test whether a deadline rejects clearly wrong rates; nonarrival tests missing progress or delivery, and offsets test whether the oracle checks the programmed target. These hand-selected operators diagnose those properties, not the prevalence of field faults. We did not vary delivery delay.

\section{Experimental Setup and Scope}
\textbf{Scope.} We purposively selected three upstream cases for visible timing windows: alarm callback, alarm pending and calendar counting. We later ran full applications with \textbf{10 distinct runnable upstream case names}, including those three, from 19 declared names: five runnable RTC API cases, four counter basic cases and one counter seconds case. Four distinct names have waiting-sensitive failures; we repaired the fourth, counter all-channels, in its full application. These are simulated board configurations, not physical boards. They share \textbf{one existing Renode STM32F4 RTC model}, so board labels do not provide independent hardware replication. Counter applications use \textbf{non-stock RTC-only configurations}, with the existing counter adapter enabled and other timer counters and the RTC API driver disabled; they do not add a second repaired driver. We retained checks that skip cases when required device features are unavailable. Neither this selection nor the repair subset estimates developer-test prevalence.

\textbf{Execution.} Renode v1.17.0 is an instruction-level embedded simulator~\cite{renodetime}. Each run starts a fresh process with unchanged source/ELF, CPU, SysTick and synchronization quantum within its variant. The prespecified schedule has 100 shared integer-frequency settings plus two endpoints within the documented interval. Build, five nominal passes and calibration precede production; jobs ran on TACC Lonestar6. The harness sets Renode's existing RTC model parameter \texttt{wakeupTimerFrequency} in generated platform files without changing model code; fidelity to silicon remains unvalidated. Later jobs ran the counter variants on the same schedule, diagnostic replays and references (never replacing a primary verdict), an 11-process rate check and the sweep in Section~\ref{sec:sweep}.

F4, F767 and F746 use 17--47 kHz; F767 is explicitly an F4 configuration replica. H753 uses the DS12117 Rev 10 revision-V envelope, 29.760--33.600 kHz at VDD 1.62--3.6 V and junction $-40$ to $105\,^{\circ}$C~\cite{stm32h753datasheet}. F746 counter basic is excluded after an MPU fault, F072 after no test output, and watchdog after no demonstrated reboot. The F072 divider observation remains unconfirmed.

\textbf{Attribution and missingness.} Same-input failure replays, exact source lines and task-specific references (separate tests of the intended behavior with bounded waits) distinguish waiting defects, phase-dependent waits, cleanup cascades and unresolved rate contracts. References check premature behavior and progress under their registered ceiling; a passing progress reference cannot override a failed rate reference. Capability skips and missing verdicts remain separate from passes. Interrupted executions were recovered under a prespecified rule; finalized harness errors remain missing.

\textbf{Repair cohort.} We fixed the original roster after the initial clock study and before repair builds: three isolated upstream cases plus 30 generated suites with identifiable numeric wait budgets (27 counting, three pending), with no generated callback suite. This selection favors identifiable waiting budgets and is not a general repair-success sample. A seeded slot is one subject at one of the 100 reproducibly sampled frequencies; endpoints are the legal 17/47-kHz controls. Each variant has 3,300 seeded legal slots and 66 endpoints; alarm offsets apply to five alarm subjects. We pair only subjects evaluable in both variants. We report the later counter extension separately as one additional upstream case in a full application.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Fourth upstream case: legal repair and divergent fault response}\label{sec:counter}
The full F4 counter application exposes a distinct waiting defect in \texttt{counter\_basic.test\_all\_channels}. The source requests a two-tick relative alarm, waits 1.5 times its nominal conversion (3 s), then requires one callback. That assertion fails at 44 of 102 tested legal frequencies, including 17 kHz, and passes at the nominal 32 kHz and 47 kHz. The driver may add a compensating tick; at 17 kHz, three ticks ideally need 5.647 s before callback delivery. The prespecified 13-s extension, 7.648-s right-sized wait and 7.648-s polling deadline each pass the same 102 settings (Table~\ref{tab:counter}). This is \textbf{one additional upstream case}, not 306 independent repairs or a second driver.

The full application does not become failure-free. The original all-channels failure skipped the later short-relative case at 44 settings. Once the repaired wait lets it run, short-relative fails at 14 settings. The later single-shot case fails at 15 settings after repair, down from 44 original failures: 29 original failures disappear on paired runs, while 15 remain. These are not additional repaired tests. Seventeen full-application replays match the primary traces, and clean-state references cover all 87 residual failing slots.

The selected 10-kHz verdict is fail in V0, pass in V1, and fail in V1b/V2; all four 64-kHz selected probes pass, and same-input replays agree. We specified a separate rate control before execution that brackets two counter reads with independent SysTick timestamps, roughly ten seconds apart. All five nominal, three slow and three fast fresh processes meet their distinct prespecified tick-count bands: the observed deltas are 10, 3 and 20 ticks, respectively. Under the pinned model, this qualifies the three injected rates and the selected-case slow-fault outcome: \textbf{V1 loses detection, while V1b/V2 retain it in this execution context}. Replay agreement verifies reproducibility, not other phases. An earlier five-tick control measures 16.040 s at 10 kHz, \textbf{18 ms above its prespecified bound}; we preserve that unexplained failure.

\subsection{Deadline sweep at the natural phase}\label{sec:sweep}
To measure the trade-off beyond one probe, a later prespecified job rebuilt the full counter application with seven waits that differ only at the selected wait site: the original 3 s, V1b's fixed and V2's polling 7.648 s, and fixed 6, 8, 10 and 13 s. Other source is byte-identical to upstream; the ELFs are fresh builds. Each arm ran once at 4--14 kHz in 1-kHz steps and at 16, 17 and 32 kHz: 98 fresh primary processes. 17 and 32 kHz are legal controls; the 12 lower rates are out-of-envelope slow faults. Beforehand, 35 nominal full applications (five per arm) passed the selected case; afterwards, one 10-kHz replay per arm matched its primary case vector, raw UART and normalized trace. No selected verdict is missing (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}).

Before execution we classified every cell by its ideal band as all-phase accept, all-phase reject or phase-dependent. All 38 predicted rejections fail and all 38 predicted acceptances pass. All 22 phase-dependent cells also fail, so each observed transition lies at the upper edge of its band: arrival behaved close to the full three-tick ideal delay at every sampled rate. We did not measure phase and cannot separate it from setup or delivery delay. Because this edge favors slow-fault detection, the brackets are likely optimistic for other phases.

Detection falls monotonically as the wait grows: the arms accept 0, 1, 3, 3, 4, 6 and 8 of the 12 slow rates. The 10-kHz outcomes reproduce Section~\ref{sec:counter} in fresh builds: 13 s accepts, and both 7.648-s arms reject. Right-sizing still accepts 13, 14 and 16 kHz, all below the legal minimum. Every lengthened wait passes 17 and 32 kHz, whereas the original fails 17 kHz. The 6-s wait accepts only 16 kHz, but it leaves 0.35 s over the ideal 17-kHz three-tick arrival and was tested only at 17 and 32 kHz rather than on the 102-setting schedule. It shows the cost of V1b's two-second allowance, not a validated repair. Across all cases of the 98 applications, 147 pass, 185 fail and 746 skip: edited-case success does not repair the application. The seven arms are variants of one subject.

Two earlier attempts are retained. A controlled-phase qualification (84 processes) placed the alarm at prescribed phases without changing nominal outcomes, but 9 of 42 timing diagnostics arrived outside the prespecified tolerance around the ideal delay, up to 0.29 s late. We did not widen that tolerance, so the planned controlled-phase sweep did not run. A first natural-phase attempt lost its records to a task-metadata defect and was rerun with identical sources and builds; its outcomes remain missing.

\subsection{Six subjects: preservation expected from the deadlines}
\textbf{Each of the six subjects passes the tested legal settings after every repair and retains its measured slow-fault detection.} Table~\ref{tab:subjects} shows the three upstream subjects and three generated pending suites separately. Every repaired subject passes all 102 legal settings with zero missing verdicts. Naive extension already achieves this result. The 10-kHz target duration exceeds these deadlines, so preservation is expected from the arithmetic; these six subjects do not distinguish the repair strategies at that probe.

\subsection{Analytical boundaries and unmeasured fault regions}
For the ideal ten-second target, a latest-arrival deadline $D$ accepts arrival when $320{,}000/f\leq D$. With $D=20.824$ s, the predicted boundary is about 15.367 kHz. Thus a clock below the legal 17-kHz minimum can still be accepted: fixing legal-clock sensitivity does not guarantee detection immediately outside the envelope. Phase, service stages and delivery can shift the actual boundary.

The counter's two- to three-tick arrival gives the phase-dependent bands in Table~\ref{tab:sweep}; at 10 kHz the 7.648-s deadline lies between the 6.4-s and 9.6-s ideal arrivals, so its detection is phase-conditional.

A latest-arrival-only check cannot reject an event merely for arriving too early. Existing early checks reject only violations of their own lower bound; they need not distinguish a moderately fast clock. All measured 64-kHz probes are accepted. We neither claim general fast-fault detection nor add an unevaluated early-arrival repair.

V1b detects 32/32 evaluable slow faults: the 32 common V1/V1b pairs retain 21 detections, gain 11 and lose none. All 11 gains are generated counting subjects with 102 V0 legal passes and no missing legal verdicts. They correct over-padding; they are \emph{not} the six sensitive subjects in Table~\ref{tab:subjects}.

\textbf{Worst-case missingness.} Assigning each missing repair outcome either pass or fail leaves the six-subject and counter-case headlines unchanged: their relevant legal and slow-fault pairs are complete. \subsection{Polling and added alarm checks do different work}
\textbf{Polling adds no paired slow-fault detection, while extra alarm checks detect a previously accepted programming error.} V2 detects the slow fault in 33/33 subjects, but all 32 common V1b/V2 pairs already detect it under V1b. Polling instead shortens the upstream callback's median seeded whole-test duration from 42.0 s (V1b) to 20.75 s (V2). The pooled median is 11.0 s for every variant and hides this difference; whole-test time is not callback latency.

V0--V2 detect each alarm-offset sign in 4/5 applicable subjects. V3 reaches 5/5 for each sign by adding detection in the upstream callback. Readback and callback-calendar checks were introduced together; these records cannot isolate timestamp checking's effect. Within this original 33-subject cohort, no detected fault becomes accepted between adjacent variants on common-evaluable pairs. This excludes the separate counter extension, where V1 does lose detection. Nonarrival is detected in 32/32 evaluable V0 subjects and 33/33 after repair; its missing V0 verdict is not a paired gain. No evaluable fast-oscillator probe is detected (0/32 for V1b, 0/33 otherwise). Preservation of this measured fault set therefore does not establish a complete oracle.

\subsection{Secondary generated-test context}
Generated tests cover only the original callback, pending and counting tasks: 66 of 90 attempts from three models and two prompts pass build, functional and repeated nominal gates. Without a same-spec human baseline, oracle differences do not establish model superiority; details remain in the artifact and do not support the repair claim.

\subsection{Fixed-setting repeatability}\label{sec:repeat} We reran unchanged full applications in 30 fresh processes per cell. The F4 callback fails 30/30 at 23.673 kHz and passes 30/30 at 23.847 and 32 kHz. F4 counter all-channels fails 30/30 at 29.143 kHz and passes 30/30 at 29.352 and 32 kHz. All 180 outcomes are explicit and agree with archived same-frequency vectors. This pilot shows no mixed verdict within these six cells; it does not establish a population flakiness rate or rule out rare nondeterminism.

\paragraph{Answers to the research questions.}
\textbf{RQ1:} The edited counter case and six sensitive subjects pass their tested legal settings after repair, but the counter application retains downstream failures. \textbf{RQ2:} Deadline choice changes measured slow-fault detection, and longer counter waits accept monotonically more slow rates; polling supplies the 42.0-to-20.75-s callback duration reduction, and added checks improve alarm-offset detection. \textbf{RQ3:} All 76 sweep cells predicted to be all-accept or all-reject match the derived bands; all 22 phase-dependent cells reject, placing sampled boundaries at the latest-arrival edge. No controlled-phase sweep establishes phase-robust detection.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\textbf{What the repair evidence supports.} The counter case shows a qualified slow-fault loss under naive extension but not right-sizing/polling, and the sweep extends that probe to a sampled, monotonic deadline--detection curve at one natural phase; the six subjects retain detection. The different 11-subject counting result shows why preserving an already overlong wait can leave a weak slow-clock oracle. Right-sizing supplies those gains without polling; polling reduces time; augmented checks improve alarm-offset detection. None establishes semantic equivalence for every defect or an automatic general repair procedure.

\textbf{Clock ranges and distributions.} The HSI context study perturbed only $\pm1\%$ with an approximate CPU-throughput mapping, whereas the RTC study varies LSI over 17--47 kHz with CPU/SysTick fixed; they do not form a controlled HSI-versus-LSI comparison. Neither a sampled range nor the ST accuracy/calibration documents establishes a device-frequency population distribution~\cite{st_an5067,st_an5126,st_rm0090}.

\textbf{Practical relevance: what is established.} The pinned board DTS selects LSI for STM32F4 Discovery. Upstream RTC test manifests select eligible devices using RTC aliases and supported features. This establishes a plausible configuration, not that upstream CI runs these tests on LSI-clocked hardware. A Bridle issue reports an STM32 RTC counter timeout in device-testing QA in the later short-relative case~\cite{bridle85}: related hardware-test use, not our defect. We have not submitted a patch. Practical CI incidence and maintainer acceptance remain open questions.

\textbf{Internal validity and anomalies.} The 105 failed counter-seconds rate references come from a separate case but share the RTC model, so they may bear on fidelity. Likewise, the bracketed control corroborates coarse oscillator rate but does not resolve exact timing, and it covers only 10, 32 and 64 kHz; the sweep's other rates rely on the model parameter alone. The failed phase qualification leaves arrivals up to 0.29 s later than the ideal delay unexplained. Our headline is therefore an observed oracle trade-off in this model and execution context, not validation of its physical timing.\par\smallskip
\textbf{Practical guidance and external validity.} Derive waits from the worst-case legal clock, justify a delivery allowance, retain early-arrival checks against an independent time reference, and assert the RTC calendar/programming target rather than eventual arrival alone. Our extra two seconds is an unvalidated experimental allowance: no explicit interrupt-to-callback latency bound was found in 33 scanned files, and the sweep suggests it costs two accepted slow rates. Fast-fault blindness, purposive selection, shared model behavior and non-stock counters limit external validity. One unblinded reviewer coded source/padding; related suites and frequencies do not replicate. The artifact's $2\times$ cutoff is descriptive. Missing verdicts remain missing.

\section{Related Work}
Surveys organize flaky-test causes, detection and repair~\cite{parrysurvey}; embedded studies show the relevance of timing and environmental assumptions~\cite{strandberg2020}. Sleep-based waiting is also catalogued as the \emph{Sleepy Test} smell~\cite{sleepy}, but a sleep alone does not establish unreliability. FlakeSync repairs asynchronous flaky tests by synchronizing critical and barrier points~\cite{flakesync}. TRaf proposes wait values using code similarity and change history, with optional dynamic tuning~\cite{traf}. Our manual variants instead compare fault detection after repair. FlakeRake perturbs timing to reproduce failures~\cite{rahman2024flakerake}, whereas our controlled clock settings expose sensitivity without observed within-setting mixed verdicts. Mutation testing and oracle research motivate retaining defect sensitivity~\cite{jia2011mutation,barr2015oracle}; our injected clocks and alarm offsets cover only selected properties.

\section{Conclusion}
In one counter subject, naive wait extension removes the edited case's tested legal-clock failures while losing detection of an activated slow fault; right-sizing and polling retain that detection at the tested phase/order. A seven-wait sweep shows detection falling monotonically as the wait grows, with right-sizing still accepting three out-of-envelope rates; its sampled boundaries sit at the detection-favorable edge of the predicted bands, so phase-robust detection remains unestablished. The full application retains downstream failures. Preservation in six other sensitive subjects is expected from their deadline arithmetic, while 11 different over-padded counting subjects benefit from shorter deadlines. These results motivate checking an oracle after manually repairing a wait. They do not establish detection near every legal boundary, general fast-fault detection, hardware behavior or practical CI prevalence.
